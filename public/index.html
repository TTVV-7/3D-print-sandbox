NFC cards and fobs

Pick a shape, fill it in, print it in two halves.

1 · Shape

The fob is the one that lives on a keyring. The card is CR80, the size of a credit card, so it sits in a wallet slot.

2 · Who you are

Name, company, title, phone and email are the five every guide to business cards agrees on, and five to seven things is where a card stops being read. Leave any of them empty and the line goes away. A long one shrinks to fit; past the point where that stops being printable, put a | where it should break instead.

One word is what these are for. It is set tight, welded into a single piece and given a tab for the ring; anything the weld leaves loose — the dot of an i — is bridged on.

The words get cut clean through the plate, as large as the plate will take them. | splits them over two lines. Leave this empty and give it an SVG instead — the artwork below wins over the words either way.

3 · How it looks

Plate
120 mm
60 mm
Margin
8 mm

The plate left round the cut: what you hold, and what stops the overspray. The artwork fills whatever is left.

Bridges
1.6 mm

Every counter — the middle of an O, the triangle in an A — is an island once the cut goes through, and an island with nothing holding it falls out. Each one gets a bar of plate left across it. At 0 they come away as loose pieces.

Thickness
0.8 mm

Thin is the point: every millimetre is a millimetre of wall the paint has to reach round, which is what blurs an edge. 0.8 mm is four layers and still peels off in one piece.

Letter height
14 mm

Ring hole
5 mm

A 5 mm hole takes a split ring or a lobster clasp. Nothing at all drops the tab and leaves the bare word.

On, the word sits proud of its own outline and the print takes one filament change. Off, it is one flat solid in the body colour.

Colours

Every colour is an inlay in one thin face layer — the first 0.6 mm of each half as it prints — so a multi-material printer does all its changes in three layers and the rest of the part in the body colour. Primary is the name, phone and logo; secondary the quieter lines and the tap wording.

Body is the outline and Primary the letters on top of it. The other two are not used by a keyring.

Body is the plate. A stencil prints in one colour — the other three are not used.

Relief
flush

How far the lettering stands off the face. Flush is a smooth card with every colour in the face layer. 1.2 mm you can feel with a thumb, at the cost of a colour change on every layer of the relief.

4 · The tap side

Two halves print face down on one plate, each with half the cavity and a three-pin key so they only go together one way. Glue the flat border outside the cavity and clamp; the tag ends up sealed in the middle with nothing showing.

Millimetres, measured off your own tags. The cavity adds 0.4 mm of slack, and the fob grows if it has to.

Use | to split lines. Leave it empty for arcs alone — which is what the fob gets anyway, there is no room for text beside its cavity.

Nothing here writes the tag — that is a phone job (NFC Tools and the like). This counts the bytes it would take.

For phones that don't tap, and the one addition worth making to a modern card: a short link you control can be re-pointed without reprinting. A shorter link is a coarser code, and a coarser code prints on a bigger nozzle.
