2 · Who you are

An SVG is best — its shapes come through exactly. A PNG, JPEG or WebP works too: it is traced, its background taken away, and its colours boiled down to the few flat ones the logo is really drawn in. Until you pick one, the card shows a made-up sample. A simple logo builds in a second; a detailed illustration, with hundreds of shapes, can take up to a minute each time something changes.

Off, the rectangle or white page a logo is so often exported on is taken away and the card follows the logo itself. On, the background is part of the logo — for an app icon or a badge whose square is the point.

The logo on the back as well, right behind the front one and in the same filaments — no extra head. Turn the card over and you see its mirror image, filling exactly the same outline. The back then has no room for the tap mark or a QR code.

Name, company, title, phone and email are the five every guide to business cards agrees on, and five to seven things is where a fob stops being read. Leave any of them empty and the line goes away. A long one shrinks to fit; past the point where that stops being printable, put a | where it should break instead.

The pet's name goes on the front, as big as the tag takes it. The phone number is always set in the plain bold sans whatever face the name is in, and the fit shrinks the name first and the number last — a long number is split over two lines when that lets it come out bigger. Put a | in it to choose the break yourself. The extra line is for “I'm microchipped”, a second number or a street; leave it empty and it goes away.

One word is what these are for. It is set tight, welded into a single piece and given a tab for the ring; anything the weld leaves loose — the dot of an i — is bridged on.

3 · How it looks

Size
70 mm

The longest side of the card, not counting the keyring tab. A business card is 85 mm across; 60 to 80 still reads as a card and fits a wallet.

Border round the logo
2 mm

Follows the logo: every colour in it is traced and merged, grown by the border and cut out along that line, like a die-cut sticker. Pieces that stand apart are bridged until it is one card. A long thin wordmark may leave no room for the chip — give it a rounded rectangle or a disc behind it instead.

Each colour of the logo is its own filament, inlaid 0.6 mm into the front and flush with it. A four-head printer has one head for the card and three for the logo, so a logo with more colours has its closest ones merged. A logo colour that matches the card's own — white lettering on a white card — is simply the card showing through, and costs no head at all.

A tab stands off the outline at that corner, like a keyring fob's. A hole goes through the card itself, as far into the corner as it will go while staying clear of the logo — and when there is no room for that, it becomes a tab rather than a hole through your logo. The readout says which you got.

Follows the letters: the box is the word's own outline — every letter grown by the margin, the gaps between them closed and the insides of the O's filled, so the sign is one rounded shape hugging the word, the way a channel-letter sign is. The two sliders below are then the most room the word can have, and the box comes out as big as the word needs. Rectangle is the plain box, and the one the wall mounts fit.

Size it to what it goes on. A cup or a cake makes the stencil a disc a little wider than the rim, so it rests there instead of falling in, and the artwork is fitted to the opening — dust cocoa over a cappuccino, or icing sugar over a cake. The preview stands it on the thing, to scale, so you can see how big it really is. Sizes are the usual ones; measure yours and pick a round plate if it is different.

The unlit border round the lettering. It never goes under the wall plus a millimetre: a letter out there would be cut into the wall itself, where there is no diffuser behind it to light it or to hold it on.

Bridges
1.6 mm

Every counter — the middle of an O, the triangle in an A — is an island once the cut goes through, and an island with nothing holding it falls out. Each one gets a bar of plate left across it. At 0 they come away as loose pieces.

Thickness
0.8 mm

Thin is the point: every millimetre is a millimetre of wall the paint has to reach round, which is what blurs an edge. 0.8 mm is four layers and still peels off in one piece.

Depth
24 mm

Front face to back, and so how far the light has to spread before it reaches the diffuser. Under about 12 mm of clear air you can count the LEDs through the letters; the readout says how much is left once the face and the lid have taken theirs.

Diffuser
0.8 mm

The translucent sheet printed across the whole inside of the face. It is what turns a row of LEDs into an evenly lit word — and it is also what holds the middle of an O on, so unlike a stencil there is nothing here to bridge.

Walls
2 mm

Five lines of a 0.4 mm nozzle at the default: stiff enough to hold the lid without bowing, and opaque enough that the light leaves by the letters.

Cable width
6 mm

Right and left are as you look at the lit front. Through the back, the lead leaves by a notch in the edge of the lid at that end: open to the edge, so the cable drops in rather than being threaded, and closed into a hole by the wall once the lid is in — so nothing shows from the front. Through the bottom wall is a notch in the back edge of the wall, cut a lid deeper so the cable passes under a lid that stays whole. 0 leaves no way out.

A plate that drops into a rebate in the back and stops flush on the ledge it leaves — a friction fit, so you can get it off again to change the strip. Stick the LEDs to the inside of it; it prints that face down, which is the flat one. Off leaves the back open.

The back of this box is a lid held in by friction, so tape or a screw on the lid holds the lid — the case pulls off it and leaves the back on the wall. Both mounts here are part of the case instead: a post that rises off the inside of the face, comes up through a hole in the lid and finishes flush with the back, so the load goes into the sign and the lid still lifts off to get at the strip. Tape pads are two strips to stick foam tape to, top and bottom. Screw posts put a tube through each end with the hole countersunk into the face, so it goes up and comes down with the lid on — at the cost of two screw heads showing, out in the plain border. Both need room between the wall and the lettering, and say so if the margin is too tight.

A hanging tag has two faces: the name on the front and the way home on the back. A slide-on tag is a flat plate with a slot at each end; the strap goes in one slot, behind the plate and out of the other. Nothing dangles, so it cannot jingle, snag or fall off when a ring opens — but its back is against the dog, so everything is on the front.

Width
45 mm

Each shape starts at the width where a full phone number still comes out at a readable 3.5 mm or more: a round, tag, shield or cat head from about 32 mm, a heart or paw print from about 42, a bone at 45 and a fish at 50 — a bone's number only has the bar between its lobes, a paw's only the pad, and a fish's the body in front of its tail. Picking a shape puts this back to that shape's starting size; the readout warns when the number drops under 3.5 mm.

Slot width
3.5 mm

The slots are the collar's width plus 1.5 mm long, and this wide: the strap's thickness with room to go through at an angle. Measure the collar; nylon webbing is about 2 mm, leather 3 to 4.

A round NFC sticker sealed in the middle of the tag, holding the phone number: a phone held to the tag offers to call it. It prints in one go with one pause — the printer stops once the pocket is finished, you drop the sticker in, and the rest prints over it, so it is sealed against rain, baths and teeth, and nothing shows. The 3MF carries the pause, and the readout says the height and exactly what to write to the chip (NFC Tools and the like: add a phone number record, write, then lock it). Works with every layout — with Name on both sides, the chip is where the number lives.

Optional, on the back beside the phone number: a page with the owner's details that a finder opens with their camera. Use a short link you control, so it can be re-pointed without reprinting the tag. A code needs room — at least 38 mm of tag — and the readout says when it has been squeezed.

5 mm takes the split ring or S-hook on most collars. The plastic round it is heavier than a keyring's, because a tab that tears out is a lost tag.

Colours

Every colour is an inlay in one thin face layer — the first 0.6 mm of each half as it prints — so a multi-material printer does all its changes in three layers and the rest of the part in the body colour. Primary is the name, phone and logo; secondary the quieter lines and the tap wording.

The card's own colour, and one for each colour of the logo. A new logo starts them on its own colours — and the card on the background it came on, or white — each moved to the nearest spool on the shelf, so what you see is what can be printed. Change any of them; the back's tap mark is in the first logo colour.

Body is the outline and Primary the letters on top of it. The other two are not used by a keyring.

Body is the tag. Primary is the name on the front, Secondary the phone number and QR code on the back, and Pattern the border. PETG for a hanging tag — stiff, and it does not soften in a hot car the way PLA does. TPU for a slide-on: it bends with the collar and cannot shatter, but it is soft enough to chew, so keep it off a hanging tag on a dog that chews. Pick colours with contrast: the number has to read at arm's length.

Body is the plate. A stencil prints in one colour — the other three are not used.

Body is the case and the lid, in something opaque. Primary is the lettering and the diffuser behind it, and it wants a translucent or a white filament — they are one piece, and they are the only thing the light comes through. The other two are not used.

Flush is the default and the one to sell: the lettering is inlaid 0.6 mm into the tag, so there is nothing standing up to wear off against a collar. Raised is for the look, and only the front can be raised — the back prints face down.

Flush is a smooth card with the logo inlaid level with it. Raised stands the logo off the face — you can feel it — at the cost of a colour change on every layer of the relief.

4 · The tap side

Two parts on one plate, the card split through the middle of its thickness with half the sticker's cavity in each, and three pins on the glue line so they only go together one way. No pause: print both, put the sticker in the cavity, glue the flat round it and clamp. Use Pulled apart above the preview to see the sticker between them.

A round NTAG sticker in a pocket in the middle of the card, at its roomiest point. 4 · The tap side

Two halves print face down on one plate, each with half the cavity and a three-pin key so they only go together one way. Glue the flat border outside the cavity and clamp; the tag ends up sealed in the middle with nothing showing.

Millimetres, measured off your own tags. The cavity adds 0.4 mm of slack, and the fob grows if it has to.
